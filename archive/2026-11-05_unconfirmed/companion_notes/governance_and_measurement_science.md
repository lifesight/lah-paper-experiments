## Part 1, Q1. NIST AI RMF 1.0 (2023) and the Generative AI Profile (NIST AI 600-1, 2024): which functions and actions concern logging, provenance, human oversight and auditability?

### Takeaway
The AI RMF 1.0 Core puts documentation and human-oversight duties under GOVERN, MAP, MEASURE and MANAGE (notably GOVERN 3.2, MAP 2.2, MAP 3.5, MEASURE 2.5 to 2.9, MANAGE 4.1 and 4.3), and the GAI Profile turns provenance, tamper-proof content history, real-time auditing and human-oversight roles into numbered suggested actions (GV-1.5-001, GV-1.6-003, GV-6.1-008, MS-2.8-003, MG-2.2-002/003/007), plus a citable definition of "information integrity" that includes chain of custody and acknowledged uncertainty.

### Cited Findings

### Inferences
- The AI RMF never uses the word "audit log", but MEASURE 2.6 (real-time monitoring), MANAGE 4.1 (post-deployment monitoring with input capture) and MANAGE 4.3 (tracked incidents) together describe what an append-only decision log satisfies; the GAI Profile's MS-2.8-003 ("tamper-proof history", "traceability") is the closest NIST language to a hash-chained decision ledger.
- NIST AI 600-1's information-integrity definition maps one-to-one onto a harness that records source, evidence, uncertainty and validity window per figure; it can be cited as the normative definition of "evidence quality".

### Gaps
- The AIRC Core page returned subcategory excerpts, not every full sentence; quote the PDF when exact wording matters.
- No NIST text was found that specifically addresses agents' tool calls or decision logs; the February 2026 AI Agent Standards Initiative (see Q8) is where NIST's agent-specific work sits.

## Part 1, Q2. EU AI Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689): Articles 12, 13, 14, 50; is a marketing budget-recommendation agent high-risk?

### Takeaway
Articles 12 to 14 impose automatic event logging, deployer-facing transparency and effective human oversight on high-risk systems only; Article 50 imposes disclosure duties on any AI system that interacts directly with people or generates synthetic content. High-risk status under Article 6(2) depends on the Annex III list, whose eight areas (biometrics, critical infrastructure, education, employment, essential services, law enforcement, migration, justice/democracy) do not name marketing or budget allocation; the consolidated text of 27 July 2026 (amended by Regulation (EU) 2026/1744) moves the application date of the high-risk chapter to 2 December 2027 (Annex III) and 2 August 2028 (Annex I).

### Cited Findings

VERIFIED (read in the browser from EUR-Lex; the mirror at artificialintelligenceact.eu was read first and the EUR-Lex text matched). [EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj/eng)
- Article 12(1): "High-risk AI systems shall technically allow for the automatic recording of events (logs) over the lifetime of the system." Article 12(2): "In order to ensure a level of traceability of the functioning of a high-risk AI system that is appropriate to the intended purpose of the system, logging capabilities shall enable the recording of events relevant for: (a) identifying situations that may result in the high-risk AI system presenting a risk within the meaning of Article 79(1) or in a substantial modification; (b) facilitating the post-market monitoring referred to in Article 72; and (c) monitoring the operation of high-risk AI systems referred to in Article 26(5)." Article 12(3) adds minimum log content for Annex III point 1(a) biometric systems (period of each use, reference database, matched input data, identity of the verifying persons). [Source](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj/eng)

[Source](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj/eng)
- Article 14(1): high-risk systems "can be effectively overseen by natural persons during the period in which they are in use." Article 14(4) requires that overseers are enabled "(a) to properly understand the relevant capacities and limitations ... including in view of detecting and addressing anomalies, dysfunctions and unexpected performance; (b) to remain aware of the possible tendency of automatically relying or over-relying on the output produced by a high-risk AI system (automation bias), in particular for high-risk AI systems used to provide information or recommendations for decisions to be taken by natural persons; (c) to correctly interpret the high-risk AI system's output ...; (d) to decide, in any particular situation, not to use the high-risk AI system or to otherwise disregard, override or reverse the output ...; (e) to intervene in the operation of the high-risk AI system or interrupt the system through a 'stop' button or a similar procedure that allows the system to come to a halt in a safe state." [Source](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj/eng)
- Article 26(6) (deployer log retention): "Deployers of high-risk AI systems shall keep the logs automatically generated by that high-risk AI system to the extent such logs are under their control, for a period appropriate to the intended purpose of the high-risk AI system, of at least six months, unless provided otherwise in applicable Union or national law". Article 26(2): "Deployers shall assign human oversight to natural persons who have the necessary competence, training and authority, as well as the necessary support." Article 26(11): deployers of Annex III systems "that make decisions or assist in making decisions related to natural persons shall inform the natural persons that they are subject to the use of the high-risk AI system." [Source](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj/eng)
- Article 50(1): "Providers shall ensure that AI systems intended to interact directly with natural persons are designed and developed in such a way that the natural persons concerned are informed that they are interacting with an AI system, unless this is obvious from the point of view of a natural person who is reasonably well-informed, observant and circumspect". Article 50(2): providers of systems "generating synthetic audio, image, video or text content, shall ensure that the outputs of the AI system are marked in a machine-readable format and detectable as artificially generated or manipulated"; the duty does not apply where systems "perform an assistive function for standard editing or do not substantially alter the input data provided by the deployer or the semantics thereof". Article 50(5): information "shall be provided ... in a clear and distinguishable manner at the latest at the time of the first interaction or exposure." [Source](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj/eng)

### Inferences
- Reading the text only: a budget-recommendation agent for advertisers is not in Annex III and is not a safety component of an Annex I product, so Articles 12 to 14 do not attach to it by the Regulation's own classification; what does attach to a conversational agent is Article 50(1) (disclose that the user is interacting with AI) and, for generated text, Article 50(2) marking unless the assistive-editing exception applies. This is a reading of the text, not a legal opinion; Article 6(5) guidelines and Article 7 delegated acts can change the list.
- The paper can still cite Articles 12 and 14 as the design benchmark it voluntarily meets (lifetime event logs, traceability for post-market monitoring, automation-bias awareness, override and stop controls).

### Gaps
- Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 itself was not opened; only its effect in the consolidated text was read.
- Commission guidelines under Article 6(5) (due by 2 February 2026) were not retrieved.

## Part 1, Q3. ISO/IEC 42001:2023 scope in one line

### Takeaway
ISO/IEC 42001:2023 specifies requirements for an organisation's AI management system (AIMS), not for individual AI systems.

### Cited Findings

- ISO's own statement: "ISO/IEC 42001 is an international standard that specifies requirements for establishing, implementing, maintaining, and continually improving an Artificial Intelligence Management System (AIMS) within organizations. It is designed for entities providing or utilizing AI-based products or services, ensuring responsible development and use of AI systems." [Source](https://www.iso.org/standard/42001)
- Applicability: "Organizations of any size involved in developing, providing, or using AI-based products or services." [Source](https://www.iso.org/standard/42001)

## Part 1, Q4. OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications (2025) and OWASP Top 10 for Agentic Applications (2026): prompt injection, excessive agency, insecure output handling

### Takeaway
The 2025 LLM list defines prompt injection (LLM01), improper output handling (LLM05) and excessive agency (LLM06) with mitigations that read as harness requirements (least privilege, human approval for high-risk actions, treat model output as untrusted, complete mediation, logging); the December 2025 Agentic Top 10 (ASI01 to ASI10) re-frames these for agents as goal hijack, tool misuse, identity and privilege abuse, memory poisoning, cascading failures and human-agent trust exploitation.

### Cited Findings

## Part 1, Q5. Model Context Protocol security guidance and academic work on MCP tool poisoning

### Takeaway
The MCP specification requires explicit user consent for tool invocation, says a human SHOULD always be able to deny tool calls, declares tool annotations untrusted unless from a trusted server, and tells clients to "log tool usage for audit purposes"; its security-best-practices page forbids token passthrough and session-based authentication and asks for scope minimisation with logged elevation events. Peer-reviewed-style preprints (MCPTox, Hou et al., Radosevich and Halloran) and Invariant Labs' April 2025 disclosure document tool poisoning as hidden instructions in tool metadata, with attack success up to 72.8% and refusal rates under 3%.

### Cited Findings

Citation H: Model Context Protocol Specification 2025-06-18, "Security and Trust & Safety". VERIFIED. [Source](https://modelcontextprotocol.io/specification/2025-06-18)
- Key principles: "Users must explicitly consent to and understand all data access and operations"; "Hosts must obtain explicit user consent before invoking any tool"; "Tools represent arbitrary code execution and must be treated with appropriate caution. In particular, descriptions of tool behavior such as annotations should be considered untrusted, unless obtained from a trusted server."; "While MCP itself cannot enforce these security principles at the protocol level, implementors SHOULD: Build robust consent and authorization flows ...". [Source](https://modelcontextprotocol.io/specification/2025-06-18)

Citation I: MCP Specification 2025-06-18, Server Features: Tools. VERIFIED. [Source](https://modelcontextprotocol.io/specification/2025-06-18/server/tools)
- "For trust & safety and security, there SHOULD always be a human in the loop with the ability to deny tool invocations." Applications SHOULD "Present confirmation prompts to the user for operations, to ensure a human is in the loop". [Source](https://modelcontextprotocol.io/specification/2025-06-18/server/tools)
- "For trust & safety and security, clients MUST consider tool annotations to be untrusted unless they come from trusted servers." [Source](https://modelcontextprotocol.io/specification/2025-06-18/server/tools)

## Part 1, Q6. Audit logging and tamper evidence

### Takeaway
Schneier and Kelsey (1999) give the canonical forward-secure, hash-chained audit log in which an attacker who later seizes the machine cannot undetectably alter earlier entries; Crosby and Wallach (2009) give the Merkle-tree history log with logarithmic proofs (3 KB instead of 800 MB for an 80-million-event log) that an untrusted logger can be audited by clients.

### Cited Findings
Citation O: Schneier and Kelsey, "Secure Audit Logs to Support Computer Forensics", ACM Transactions on Information and System Security 1(3):159-176, 1999. VERIFIED (publisher-independent page and the full PDF from schneier.com; DOI 10.1145/317087.317089 not confirmed because Crossref rate-limited). [Page](https://www.schneier.com/academic/archives/1999/05/secure_audit_logs_to.html); [PDF](https://www.schneier.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/02/paper-auditlogs.pdf)
- Problem statement: sensitive information "must be kept in log files on an untrusted machine"; goal that an attacker who gains control learns little and cannot alter or destroy pre-compromise entries undetectably, by a computationally cheap method. [Source](https://www.schneier.com/academic/archives/1999/05/secure_audit_logs_to.html)
- Mechanism (from the PDF): "The log's authentication key is hashed using a one-way hash function immediately after a log entry is written. The new value of the authentication key overwrites and irretrievably deletes the previous value"; "A_j is the authentication key for the j-th entry in the log. This is the core secret that provides all of this scheme's security"; "Y_j = hash(Y_{j-1}, E_Kj(D_j), W_j). This is the hash chain which we maintain to allow partially-trusted users V to verify parts of the log over a low-bandwidth connection". [Source](https://www.schneier.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/02/paper-auditlogs.pdf)

- "This paper considers the case of an untrusted logger, serving a number of clients who wish to store their events in the log, and kept honest by a number of auditors who will challenge the logger to prove its correct behavior." "The logger must be able to prove that individual logged events are still present, and that the log, as seen now, is consistent with how it was seen in the past." "Where a classic hash chain might require an 800 MB trace to prove that a randomly chosen event is in a log with 80 million events, our prototype returns a 3 KB proof with the same semantics." [Source](https://www.usenix.org/legacy/event/sec09/tech/full_papers/crosby.pdf)

## Part 1, Q7. Event sourcing, the Saga pattern, idempotent or exactly-once processing

### Takeaway
Fowler (2005) defines event sourcing as capturing all state changes as an ordered event sequence that supports complete rebuild, temporal query and replay; Garcia-Molina and Salem (1987) define a saga as a sequence of transactions that either all complete or are undone by compensating transactions; Helland (2012) states that messages are "guaranteed to be delivered zero or more times" so processing must be idempotent; Kafka KIP-98 implements exactly-once via producer IDs with sequence numbers, atomic multi-partition transactions and read_committed consumers.

### Cited Findings

- Summary line: "Capture all changes to an application state as a sequence of events." Capabilities: Complete Rebuild ("discard the application state completely and rebuild it" by replaying), Temporal Query ("the application state at any point in time" by "starting with a blank state and rerunning the events up to a particular time or event"), Event Replay (correct a wrong past event by "reversing it and later events"). "The official system of record can either be the event logs or the current application state." [Source](https://martinfowler.com/eaaDev/EventSourcing.html)

Citation R: Garcia-Molina and Salem, "Sagas", Proceedings of the 1987 ACM SIGMOD International Conference on Management of Data, pp. 249-259, 1987, DOI 10.1145/38713.38742. VERIFIED-INDEX (Crossref record; abstract via Consensus). - "A LLT is a saga if it can be written as a sequence of transactions that can be interleaved with other transactions. The database management system guarantees that either all the transactions in a saga are successfully completed or compensating transactions are run to amend a partial execution." [Source](https://consensus.app/papers/details/aa4a0a4a183551d88b4ca086a74c87e5/)

## Part 2, Q11. Attribution versus causality; a standard statement that multi-touch attribution is not a causal estimate

### Takeaway
Dalessandro et al. (2012) position attribution as a causal estimation problem and state that when causal assumptions are violated attribution scores are only "variable importance measures"; Google's Kelly, Vaver and Koehler (2018) state that rule-based attribution never defines credit "in a principled way" and rests on requirements (credit sums to conversions, is non-negative, ignores non-converting paths) that "do not hold in practice and, in fact, undermine the task of measuring ad effectiveness"; Berman (2018) shows last-touch "overincentivize[s] ad exposures".

### Cited Findings
Citation AM: Dalessandro, Perlich, Stitelman, Provost, "Causally motivated attribution for online advertising", Proceedings of the Sixth International Workshop on Data Mining for Online Advertising and Internet Economy (ADKDD), KDD '12, pp. 1-9, 2012, DOI 10.1145/2351356.2351363. VERIFIED-INDEX (Crossref record; abstract via Consensus). [Crossref](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1145/2351356.2351363); [Abstract](https://consensus.app/papers/details/0d14d636feaf5e22ad057b185dc0b27c/)
- "we position attribution as a causal estimation problem and then propose two approximation methods as alternatives for when the full causal estimation can not be done ... We argue that in cases where causal assumptions are violated, these approximate methods can be interpreted as variable importance measures." [Source](https://consensus.app/papers/details/0d14d636feaf5e22ad057b185dc0b27c/)

Citation AN: Kelly, Vaver, Koehler (Google), "A Causal Framework for Digital Attribution", 2018. VERIFIED (PDF text-extracted). [Pub page](https://research.google/pubs/pub46905/); [PDF](https://storage.googleapis.com/gweb-research2023-media/pubtools/4415.pdf)
- "We formalize the problem of attribution under a causal framework, note its shortcomings, and suggest an attribution algorithm that is evaluated via simulation." On current practice: "the concept of credit is never defined in a principled way and so it is unclear what each algorithm is actually trying to estimate"; entrenched reporting has produced "requirements for attribution that imply underlying assumptions that do not hold in practice and, in fact, undermine the task of measuring ad effectiveness", namely that credit "must add to the total number of reported conversions", "is non-negative at the event level", "is additive", and "is not assigned to events in non-converting paths". - The paper can state, with three independent sources, that attribution credit is a bookkeeping allocation of observed conversions, not an estimate of the counterfactual, and that the harness must therefore label attribution reads as "allocation" rather than "effect".

## Part 2, Q12. Platform-reported ROAS versus incremental ROAS: the best citable evidence on the gap

### Takeaway
The strongest citable evidence is experimental: eBay's paid-search experiments found returns "a fraction of conventional non-experimental estimates" and brand-keyword ads with "no measurable short-term benefits" (Blake, Nosko, Tadelis 2015); Yahoo's experiments show "activity bias" leading observational methods to "a massive overestimate" (Lewis, Rao, Reiley 2011); the Facebook studies (Q10) quantify over-estimation of lift by observational methods; vendor figures on the platform-versus-incremental gap (1.5x to 3x, 30 to 60%) exist only in marketing material.

### Cited Findings
Citation AP: Blake, Nosko, Tadelis, "Consumer Heterogeneity and Paid Search Effectiveness: A Large-Scale Field Experiment", Econometrica 83(1):155-174, 2015 (NBER Working Paper 20171, May 2014). VERIFIED (NBER abstract page; journal citation stated there). [Source](https://www.nber.org/papers/w20171)

## Part 2, Q13. Saturation and diminishing returns: the Hill function in MMM, and adstock

### Takeaway
Jin et al. (2017) give the standard MMM forms: adstock as a weighted average over L lags with geometric decay w(l) = alpha^l (alpha the "retention rate", attributed to Hanssens, Parsons and Schultz 2003) or delayed adstock, and the Hill function Hill(x; K, S) = 1 / (1 + (x/K)^(-S)) with K the half-saturation point and S the slope, multiplied by a channel coefficient beta; Robyn uses the same Hill form with alpha (shape) and gamma (inflection). Broadbent (1979) is the conventional origin citation for adstock but could not be verified in any index on 2026-11-05.

### Cited Findings
Citation AE (again): Jin et al. 2017. VERIFIED (PDF text-extracted). [PDF](https://storage.googleapis.com/gweb-research2023-media/pubtools/3806.pdf)
- Adstock, equation (1): adstock(x_{t-L+1,m}, ..., x_{t,m}; w_m, L) = sum_{l=0}^{L-1} w_m(l) x_{t-l,m} / sum_{l=0}^{L-1} w_m(l), "where w_m is a nonnegative weight function. The cumulative media effect is a weighted average of media spend in the current week and previous L-1 weeks." Geometric decay, equation (2): w_m(l; alpha_m) = alpha_m^l, 0 < alpha_m < 1, "where we refer to alpha_m as the retention rate of ad effect of the m-th media from one period to the next", citing "(Hanssens, Parsons & Schultz, 2003)". Delayed adstock, equation (3): w_m(l; alpha_m, theta_m) = alpha_m^((l - theta_m)^2), "where theta_m is the delay of the peak effect." L = 13 weeks used as an approximation to infinity. [Source](https://storage.googleapis.com/gweb-research2023-media/pubtools/3806.pdf)
- Hill function, equation (4): Hill(x_{t,m}; K_m, S_m) = 1 / (1 + (x_{t,m}/K_m)^(-S_m)), x >= 0, "where S_m > 0 is the shape parameter which is also referred to as slope, and K_m > 0 is the half saturation point, because Hill(K_m) = 1/2 for any value of K_m and S_m. As x goes to infinity, the Hill function approaches 1." The origin is pharmacology "(Gesztelyi et al., 2012; Hill, 1910)". Shape transformation is beta_m Hill_m(x), and "the parameters of beta Hill are essentially unidentifiable in some scenarios." [Source](https://storage.googleapis.com/gweb-research2023-media/pubtools/3806.pdf)
- Jin et al. do not cite Broadbent for adstock (the extracted text contains no "Broadbent"). [Source](https://storage.googleapis.com/gweb-research2023-media/pubtools/3806.pdf)

Citation AG (again): Robyn Analyst's Guide. VERIFIED. [Source](https://facebookexperimental.github.io/Robyn/docs/analysts-guide-to-MMM)
- "Robyn utilizes the Hill function to reflect the saturation of each media channel", with alpha controlling shape and gamma the inflection point; geometric adstock has one parameter theta and "can be considered as too simple and often not suitable for digital media transformations"; Weibull adstock offers more flexible shape and scale. [Source](https://facebookexperimental.github.io/Robyn/docs/analysts-guide-to-MMM)

Citation AT: Broadbent, "One Way TV Advertisements Work", Journal of the Market Research Society, 1979, commonly cited as vol. 23 no. 3, pp. 139-166 (some sources give vol. 21). UNVERIFIED (2026-11-05): not present in Crossref's JMRS records; Semantic Scholar API rate-limited; Wikipedia's entry attaches a DOI from a different journal. [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Advertising_adstock)
- Verifiable alternatives by the same author: Broadbent and Fry, "Adstock Modelling for the Long Term", Journal of the Market Research Society 37(4):1-18, 1995, DOI 10.1177/147078539503700404; Broadbent, "Advertising Effects: More than Short Term", JMRS 35(1):1-11, 1993, DOI 10.1177/147078539303500103. VERIFIED-INDEX (Crossref). [Crossref](https://api.crossref.org/works?query.bibliographic=Broadbent+One+way+TV+advertisements+work+Journal+of+the+Market+Research+Society&rows=4)

### Inferences
- For the paper, cite Jin et al. 2017 for both the adstock and Hill forms and Hanssens, Parsons and Schultz (2003) for geometric decay; cite Broadbent 1979 only with the volume caveat, or substitute Broadbent and Fry 1995.
